A more complex and complicated word than it seems…

My students and I often have conversations about “right” and “wrong” and politically correct language in my classroom for all sorts of reasons, but usually because they are white and I am white, and we are trying to think about what that means for the language we use and the things we may not know we are carrying into a room, a classroom, our room, any room with us.

So when a faculty member suggested to me that the word stakeholder was “bad,” I started looking into it. And, yes, it hurt my feelings, because I genuinely, earnestly did not mean it in a “bad” way. But I also had to know. I had been away from academia for a long time, and I needed to—wanted desperately to—reacquire the vocabulary I had lost, rehab some of the language I had acquired in my time away, and become part of the “correct academic crowd” in our department. Plus, I’m white, and I know that matters here, there, and everywhere.

Having hurt feelings in my family was okay, by the way, and in this case having my feelings hurt by a faculty member’s comment was okay with me too, especially if the hurt made me think more reflexively about what I had said or done. In my family, though, sometimes what was wanted was not necessarily reflexivity but reflection—that I reflect my parents or my family back at themselves—and in the case of this faculty member, I am not entirely sure what was wanted. But I would like to think it was something along the lines of what I have done, or am doing, here: not just changing a word because someone told me it was wrong, but stopping long enough to ask why, where that judgment comes from, what the word carries with it, and what I may not have known when I used it. So, of course, I had to look into it, because I am the type of person who googles at 3 a.m. how tall Alexander the Great was.

Several conversations, one word

And what I realized was that there are actually several different conversations happening at the same time around the word stakeholder. One critique is about language, colonialism, power, and who gets to name whom—especially what happens when broad institutional labels flatten very different legal, cultural, political, and historical relationships (Reed et al. 1482–86; Terminology Standardization Division).

Another critique comes out of business, economics, and corporate governance and asks a different question: does stakeholder theory actually do what it says it does? Bebchuk and Tallarita, for example, question whether giving corporate leaders broad discretion to balance all of these competing stakeholder interests actually produces meaningful benefits or accountability for the people stakeholder governance claims to protect (96–101).

And then there is an environmental critique that asks a sort of speculative fiction based question: what happens when the thing supposedly being represented as a stakeholder cannot sit at the table, negotiate, compromise, or speak back? Driscoll and Starik had already complicated the human-centered stakeholder model by arguing that the natural environment itself should be understood as the firm’s “primary and primordial stakeholder” (55–73). More recently, David et al. push that problem further, arguing that stakeholder theory remains fundamentally anthropocentric because it depends on managerial balancing and negotiation even though ecological thresholds, planetary boundaries, and the more-than-human world cannot simply be negotiated away as competing interests (3377–78).

What interests me is that all of them eventually make me ask something similar: what gets hidden when we put a whole bunch of very different people, relationships, rights, responsibilities, and even environments under one convenient word—stakeholder? The postcolonial and/or politically correct critique is not simply an argument about whether a word is polite enough or whether we can substitute a nicer word. It’s a question about what language asks us to see and what it makes easier not to see. Then what Reed and his coauthors effectively point out is that stakeholder has become a catch-all term for almost everybody: members of the public, research participants, employees, funders, policymakers, organizations, communities, and sometimes even nonhuman species. They identify many different definitions of the term, which immediately makes me wonder how useful a supposedly precise organizational word can be if it can mean almost anyone or anything (Reed et al. 1482–83). All of one kind is no kind, just as one of a kind is no kind. And so for me, this becomes especially complicated when we are talking about Indigenous peoples.

So, if we call an Indigenous Nation a stakeholder, we can make its relationship to land, law, government, history, and sovereignty sound like an interest—as if an Indigenous nation is simply one interested group among several interested groups. But while First Peoples may be rights-holders, title-holders, treaty partners, or sovereign nations, those relationships are not interchangeable with the interests of a corporation, a developer, an advocacy organization, or even a neighboring community (“Rethinking the Word ‘Stakeholder’”).

When an interest is not a right

What looks like inclusive language can actually flatten difference, so does it matter what language we use? And is there better language out there that we can use? I have often had this debate with myself, but the answer is yes. Language that aligns with how someone sees themselves is indeed better language, and language that does not flatten and smooth out difference into sameness, is also better. In other words, language is complex, complicated, and contextual and the more we understand it that way—less neutrally, the more of a full picture we can get if we are truly trying to understand something particularly if it’s from somebody else’s point of view.

For instance, if we say that an Indigenous nation, a corporation, a local government, an environmental organization, and a neighborhood association are all “stakeholders,” the grammar itself places them beside one another as though they enter the situation on roughly equal terms. But they do not necessarily have equal power, equal vulnerability, equal legal standing, equal histories, or equal relationships to the place being discussed. And here I think, is where the postcolonial critique becomes much more important than simply saying the word has a “bad history.”

The history of the word itself is actually complicated [1]. What happens when the language of stakes, claims, ownership, interest, negotiation, and exchange gets carried into relationships already shaped by colonial dispossession? Canada’s government Language Portal makes that similar distinction when it argues that Indigenous Peoples should not automatically be described as stakeholders because their relationship may be based on constitutionally protected rights rather than simply an interest in a project (“Rethinking the Word ‘Stakeholder’”). Reed and his coauthors likewise argue that stakeholder terminology can reduce complicated relationships between people and place into something resembling economic or transactional interests (1482–84). Having an interest in something is not necessarily the same thing as having rights in relation to something. Those are very different rhetorical positions.

There is also the older gambling meaning of stakeholder. A stakeholder could be the supposedly “neutral” person holding the money or property wagered by other people until the outcome of the bet was decided (Reed et al. 1483–84). Laridae’s Danielle Rocheleau draws attention to this history as another reason the contemporary term doesn’t feel quite right either: having something “at stake” does not necessarily mean having any control over what happens to it (Rocheleau).

But what bothers me too is that organizations use the word stakeholder engagement as though the word itself signals participation. Does it?

You can have something at stake and still have very little power. You can be profoundly affected by a decision without being allowed to make the decision. You can be consulted without having the right to say no. You can even be “engaged” without having any meaningful power to change the outcome. So maybe the problem is not simply the word, but maybe the problem is how easily the word can allow consultation to sound like participation, participation to sound like power, and interest to sound like rights.

Say whom we mean

There is also an important counterargument here, though, because simply replacing stakeholder with another word does not necessarily fix any of this, unless it does because it is more aligned and does not flatten and smooth out difference into sameness. Don Enright argues that stakeholder can still be useful precisely because it does not pretend that everyone involved is a partner. Calling someone a “partner” when that person has no shared authority, no shared risk, and no real decision-making power can be more misleading than calling that person a stakeholder (Enright). I do think that’s worth thinking about though because replacing a problematic word with a more pleasant word while leaving the power relationship exactly as it was/is does not accomplish anything.

So, some organizations have moved toward rights-holders, particularly for Indigenous nations and communities with legal, treaty, territorial, or other recognized rights. Other alternatives include interested parties, affected communities, community members, partners, collaborators, or interest-holders (Akl et al.; Phipps). But Reed et al. make what I think is the more useful move: rather than looking for another universal category that will replace stakeholder, they suggest actually naming the people and relationships involved and allowing people, wherever possible, to identify themselves (1485–86). Simple. Instead of saying stakeholders, say whom we mean: Employees, Tribal nations. Community members. Residents. Customers. Funders. Government agencies. Researchers. Landowners.

People whose water is being used.

People whose homes are beside the development.

People whose treaty rights are implicated.

People who make money from the decision.

People who bear the environmental consequences of the decision.

Once we begin naming the actual relationships, it becomes much harder to pretend everybody is standing in the same place. True. But saying whom we mean can be problematic too.

Responsibility to everyone. Accountable to whom?

Still there is a whole other aspect of stakeholder and Stakeholder Theory itself that we have yet to address and discuss. R. Edward Freeman’s 1984 Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach developed stakeholder theory as an alternative to the idea that corporations exist primarily to maximize value for shareholders. Stakeholder theory expands corporate responsibility outward toward employees, customers, suppliers, governments, communities, and others affected by the organization (Ajani and Olorunfemi 1511–12). And I can see why that is appealing. It seems ethically better to say that a corporation has responsibilities to employees and communities and customers rather than simply to whoever owns shares in it.

But again I keep coming back to the same question: how? How exactly does a corporation balance all of these interests? If employees want higher wages, investors want higher returns, customers want lower prices, communities want environmental protections, and executives claim responsibility to all of them, who gets prioritized? According to what principle? And who holds the corporation accountable when those interests conflict?

Ajani and Olorunfemi identify precisely these difficulties: deciding who counts as a stakeholder, determining how conflicting interests should be prioritized, establishing meaningful measures of performance, and preventing managerial discretion from becoming a way of avoiding accountability (1517–18). That accountability problem is also central to Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita’s “The Illusory Promise of Stakeholder Governance.” They argue that giving corporate leaders broad discretion to claim that they are balancing stakeholder interests does not necessarily protect employees, communities, customers, or other affected groups. It may instead give corporate managers more freedom to explain almost any decision as serving some stakeholder interest (Bebchuk and Tallarita).

So when corporations say they are practicing “stakeholder capitalism,” that statement by itself does not tell us who actually has power and/or where the power is. So again, I find myself asking almost the same questions I was asking in the postcolonial discussion: Who gets named? Who gets heard? Who gets to decide? Who can refuse? Who bears the consequences? Who can hold whom accountable? And how?

Further, Phillips, Freeman, and Wicks complicate this criticism in an important way because their article “What Stakeholder Theory Is Not” is not actually an argument that stakeholder theory should simply be abandoned. They acknowledge that the conceptual breadth of the term stakeholder can create problems, but their purpose is to clarify the boundaries of stakeholder theory rather than declare the theory a failure (479). So there is a real debate here about whether stakeholder theory is fundamentally flawed or whether it has simply been used too loosely that everyone should know about.

And who speaks for the river?

And finally, there is the environmental problem, which seems to push this all even further. Traditional stakeholder models usually place the organization at the center and then arrange its stakeholders around it—employees, customers, investors, governments, communities, and sometimes “the environment” (Ajani and Olorunfemi 1520). But what does it mean to make the environment one stakeholder among several? A river cannot negotiate. An ecosystem cannot agree to a compromise. A climate threshold cannot decide that maybe exceeding it by another ten percent is acceptable because doing so will preserve quarterly earnings. Or can it? Is it as simple as that? David and his coauthors make this ecocentric critique directly, arguing that conventional stakeholder theory remains too anthropocentric because it assumes that environmental concerns can be negotiated alongside other human interests. Ecological thresholds and planetary boundaries, however, are material constraints and more than that, rather than preferences that managers can bargain away (David et al.).

And suddenly the language problem comes back again. Who speaks for the river? Who speaks for the land? Who gets to decide that environmental damage is an acceptable trade? And perhaps more importantly: why and how does the corporation remain at the center of the model in the first place?

What I keep coming back to

So after going through all of this, I do not think my conclusion is that stakeholder is necessarily “a bad word.” Because, it is doing rhetorical work and telling us sort of who it is and where it’s coming from, but I think using bad flattens and smooths some of the work it is doing out too much. And I do not think my conclusion is that stakeholder theory simply failed, although it has failed in spectacular ways. Still, both conclusions feel too easy to me. What I do keep coming back to instead are questions of naming, relation, and power: are we calling people what they want to be called? What gets flattened or smoothed over when we put First Peoples and Indigenous Peoples, employees, corporations, communities, ecosystems, and even a river into the same category of stakeholder? Who gains power from that flattening, and who loses it?

And what kind of solidarity is possible if the language we use to gather people together erases the very differences—rights, histories, vulnerabilities, responsibilities, sovereignty, relationships to land—that matter?

This is also where sensitive rhetorics enters for me, because sensitivity here is not simply about being polite or choosing the least offensive word. It is about paying attention to relation and difference, to who is exposed, who is vulnerable, who has power, who does not, and what our language does to those relations.

Maybe solidarity does not require us to smooth those differences out at all.

Maybe solidarity actually requires us to notice them, name them, and still remain in relation with them. And maybe that is the problem I keep circling around with stakeholder: not just that the word might always be wrong, but that it can make unequal relationships sound equal, turn rights into interests, make those most affected by a decision sound like one more voice at the table—and even make a river sound as though it agreed to be there.

If I call an Indigenous, First Peoples nation or an employee or an investor or a resident or a government agency or a customer, or a river all “stakeholders,” have I actually explained their relationships—or have I made those relationships disappear?

So the better question might not be: Who are our stakeholders? But maybe: Who exactly are we talking about? What is their relationship to this decision? Who has rights? Who has interests? Who has responsibilities? Who has power? Who is affected but has almost no power? Who can say no? Who cannot simply walk away? Who benefits? Who pays? Who—or what—cannot speak within the system we have built at all? And how? How does it all work and what work is it doing?

That seems like the shift I am interested in highlighting at least for now. Not necessarily finding one nicer word to replace stakeholder, which might just ultimately take us back to the same place we were/are, but refusing to let one word do so much rhetorical work that it makes unequal relationships appear equal, turns rights into interests, makes consultation look like power, or allows actual responsibility to disappear into the pleasantly vague language of “engagement.” Language things are always more complex and complicated than they seem.

Note

1. The etymological history of stakeholder is contested and more complicated than the claim that the word originally referred to European settlers literally driving stakes into First Peoples land. Stake has a long material history referring to a post driven into the ground, and expressions such as stake a claim became associated with marking and asserting claims to land. Stakeholder, however, also has a documented seventeenth-century legal and gambling history in which a stakeholder was a “neutral” person who held money or property wagered by others until a dispute or contest was resolved (Reed et al. 1483–84). Again, I question neutral here, is anyone holding an Others money entirely neutral, ever? But still Reed does caution us against treating the colonial explanation as the single definitive etymology of stakeholder. This looking for “pure” sources of definitions is problematic (cf Derrida). But Reed’s etymological qualification does not erase the postcolonial critique of the term. Rather, it distinguishes the historical origin of the word from the ways the language of stakes, claims, ownership, interests, and exchange has subsequently operated within colonial and institutional relationships, particularly when First Peoples with distinct legal, territorial, ancestral, or sovereign rights are categorized merely as one group of “stakeholders” among others (Reed et al. 1482–86; “Rethinking the Word ‘Stakeholder’”) To me etymology and rhetorical consequence are not the same question necessarily.

For instance, the postcolonial critique is not so weak that it collapses merely because the historical genealogy of one word is messier than a single origin story. Its strength lies in the question of what happens when rights become interests and unequal relationships get linguistically flattened? And, that importantly tops (dare I say that?) western-based historical genealogy and tradition to me particularly given my context.
