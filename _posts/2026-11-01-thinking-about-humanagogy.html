Recently, I was looking for a word. I was revising my teaching statement. I’ll soon be applying for jobs again—back on the academic job market after returning to doctoral study at fifty-five—and I’m trying to explain how the teacher I am now differs from, and may still be the same as, the teacher I was then. I’m also trying to connect both to the teacher I still hope to become. So, it’s a “new” teaching statement—and an assignment for my teaching colloquium, since I’m a graduate teaching assistant again.

Luckily, I already had one—or, actually, two. I had a “Pedagogy: Teaching Philosophy” and an “Andragogy: Adult Teaching Philosophy,” so I considered turning in one or the other and calling it good. Between the two, I should have been covered. But I paused.

What was I trying to say? What did I want—or, more than want, need? I needed a different word. Was there, or had there ever been, a word that covered both pedagogy and andragogy? Would it ever be possible to bundle my professional life together in a neat, tidy little package?

Graduate school can do that to you: make you look for new words, ponder things. I’d been using pedagogy and andragogy for years. It was the years that gave me pause—so many years. Surely there was a word that could make room for all those years and identities. A quick Google search, and I found humanagogy.

I liked it immediately because human, humane, humanity, and humanities were all right there in it. It also turns out that I’m somewhat late to this particular conversation—a familiar plight for the graduate student attempting to enter Burke’s parlor. Russell S. Knudson had published “Humanagogy Anyone?” in 1979. I was only eight then, but I wonder whether I should have encountered the idea as an undergraduate or in one of my two Masters degrees.

Knudson was responding to divisions within adult education. He described competing positions associated with pedagogy, andragogy, and gerogogy: approaches concerned, respectively, with children, adults, and older adults. His concern was that these divisions were becoming factions, each defending its own territory. Humanagogy offered a way to consider their contributions together. He described it as “a theory of learning that takes into account the differences between people of various ages as well as their similarities” (261). Humanagogy asks educators to consider differences without making age the whole explanation and without suggesting that everyone learns in the same way.

For me, humanagogy offers a different starting point from pedagogy or andragogy, though it may require a little more work on all of our parts. Instead of beginning with a category—child, adult, older adult—it begins with a person whose experiences continue to shape learning. It begins not just with the student, but also with the teacher—or, in other words, it includes me. Knudson calls this a “process theory,” emphasizing connections between earlier and later experiences (262).

It recognizes that both adult learners and adult educators bring childhood and other histories with them. Their experiences as children, young adults, adults, and aging adults mattered then, and continues to matter; neither learners nor educators begin from scratch when they enter a “new” classroom. Knudson gives a great example of an eighty-year-old returning to a craft first encountered at ten, bringing together the experience, technological knowledge, and wisdom acquired in the intervening years (263). An earlier interest meets later experience, and with wisdom meets application and enjoyment. Something familiar–old becomes new and possible in a different way(s)—a description of learning that resonates with Elizabeth Ellsworth’s account in Teaching Positions (158–59, 172–73).

Humanagogy can also help educators think across age groups, write across the lifespan and life cycles, recognize connections among learning experiences, and choose support according to the person and situation. An experienced adult might need careful guidance with an unfamiliar task, while a younger learner might bring considerable knowledge—even wisdom—to it. Age may matter, but perhaps not always in the ways we assume. Learning is more complex than linear binaries.

But, like all words, this one has its limits too. Putting human at the beginning does not automatically make teaching humane. Nor does humanagogy tell me exactly what to assign, how to assess learning, or when to offer more direction. Those decisions still require careful attention and thoughtful application.

Still, I like having a word that reminds me to look beyond the current category and consider a person’s history, circumstances, and possibilities—while also asking me to consider my own. For someone who has taught different learners for more than twenty years and continues to be a learner herself, that’s a lot of time, space, distance, and meaning to fit under one heading.

Humanagogy, anyone? Yes, I think so—at least for Russell Knudson then, and me right now.
