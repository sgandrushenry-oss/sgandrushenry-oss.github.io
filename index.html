CONNECTED RESEARCH PROJECT · IN PROGRESS

Literacy as Craft

What changes when we understand reading and writing as things people make? This project brings together three related manuscripts, each asking a different question about craft, literacy, and learning.

Three articles. One shared concern: recognizing the work behind meaning-making.

01Reading as craftPatch-reading & crafted reading notes

Reading is material, recursive, and social work. This manuscript explores patch-reading and crafted reading notes as ways to make that work visible before it becomes polished writing.

Reading maps, layered annotations, and visual responses give students something they can revisit, explain, and revise—and give teachers a fuller picture of meaning-making.

Patch-reading attends to the fragments readers gather, the connections they try, and the arrangements they return to. Those patches hold traces of thinking in process.

Manuscript in development

02Craft in open-access classroomsTwo-year colleges & community colleges

Craft is not easier work. This manuscript considers how making, arranging, annotating, and revising can help students participate in demanding academic work while drawing on the literacies they already bring.

The focus is on two-year and open-access institutions, where clear expectations, material access, and respect for students’ lives belong at the center of course design.

Manuscript in development

03A framework for literacy as craftMateriality, circulation & solidarity

The conceptual strand of the project approaches texts as made things and literacy as skilled, distributed activity. It asks how craft makes labor visible, brings materials and people into relation, and builds solidarity through shared work.

Conceptual manuscript in development

DESIGN & TECHNOLOGY

From Course Architecture
to Course Pathways

Rethinking Canvas Navigation in Writing Instruction

A course can contain everything students need and still make the relationships among those materials difficult to see.

Read project overview

Using a hybrid first-year writing course as a bounded mini-case, I examine how students navigate among a major project, weekly modules, learning tasks, resources, and composing spaces. Rhetorical and document-design analysis asks what students must see, remember, infer, retrieve, and coordinate.

I call the connective design work that makes these relationships visible and usable pathway architecture. The project distinguishes productive composing complexity from avoidable coordination work and proposes an “If / Then Also” approach: pedagogical choices bring corresponding responsibilities for access.

04 / GRADUATE EDUCATION & MENTORING

Graduate Students with(in) the Imaginary

Desire, Time, Labor, and Becoming Otherwise in Graduate Education

Who does graduate education imagine its students are? What are they taught to desire? And how can mentoring support their becoming without deciding beforehand who they must become?

Read project overview

I study how institutional and disciplinary expectations shape graduate students’ identities, desires, labor, and imaginable futures. Students arrive with knowledge, histories, relationships, and obligations that exceed the academic selves programs may picture for them.

My interest in graduate mentoring centers on intellectual companionship: listening, recognizing existing expertise, making room for uncertainty, and helping writers develop questions into sustainable work. I’m interested in mentoring that supports multiple futures, including community college teaching, rather than treating one career route as the measure of success.

05 / WRITING, TIME & LIFECYCLING

And the World Spins Madly On

Graduate Education as Liminal Experience

I returned to graduate school at 55, but neither “finish” nor “start over” quite describes it. I came back carrying years of teaching, writing, work, family, and change. The institution and the discipline had been changing too.

Read about lifecycling

I’m exploring writing and intellectual life through lifecycling: recursive, interrupted, and open to return, revision, and movement across a lifetime. A new genre can make an experienced writer a beginner again. An old question can take a new shape. A pause can carry work that institutional timelines do not recognize.

This strand asks what becomes possible when we understand academic lives as overlapping patches of experience rather than a single upward career arc. Graduate education involves multiple, unequal, moving thresholds. The route may be mapped; the journey cannot be predetermined.

For teaching and mentoring, that means attending to aging, caregiving, material conditions, accumulated knowledge, and different tempos of becoming—and recognizing movement between the official milestones.

02 / TEACHING & LEARNING

Students arrive
with their
hands full.

Languages. Histories. Work. Families. Questions. And knowledges that don’t always look academic, but are.

That is where I want to begin.

I teach reading and writing as practices students can try, discuss, revise, and carry into other parts of their lives. My experience across community colleges, universities, concurrent enrollment, and online learning has made access a daily teaching responsibility.

In two-year and open-access classrooms, rigor needs clear pathways: explicit expectations, manageable stages, timely feedback, and room for students to connect academic work to what they already know.

Read together. Make thinking visible.

Reading maps, annotations, conversation, and crafted notes give us something to work with before the final draft.

Design for actual students.

Course navigation, document hierarchy, and flexible routes through an assignment are part of teaching—not just the packaging around it.

Accompany graduate writers.

My mentoring interests include recognizing the knowledge graduate students already carry, supporting intellectual risk, and making room for interruption, return, and more than one possible future. I want to help writers work with fragments and uncertainty as part of inquiry.

Write beyond the classroom.

Audience, purpose, and communicative responsibility connect composition to professional, civic, and community life.

UPWARD SPIRAL

A place for
your next idea.

Handmade notebooks, journals, and sketchbooks—made to be used, filled, and made your own.

Upward Spiral began because I could never quite find a notebook that fit how I thought, who I was, or what I wanted to make. So I started making them. Paper, glue, spiral bindings, and leftover pieces became places for ideas that didn’t fit a predetermined page.

At Upward Spiral Publishing, we believe in magic, dreams, inspiration, writing, art, customization—and most of all, paper and upward spirals.

Our paper, your possibilities

We are a small, female-owned and operated Salt Lake City business making one-of-a-kind, tactile notebooks and paper products from reclaimed, upcycled, hand-cut materials. Many begin with leftover fragments: a patch of pattern, a strip of color, a piece of paper worth saving.

Our notebooks, planners, portfolios, scrapbooks, calendars, and paper projects are meant to fit imaginative people. They make room for thoughts, art, hopes, dreams, and the things you haven’t found words for yet.

You can make the notebook you’ve always wanted with us—or we can make it for you. We can gather materials and work together on a custom journal, planner, or paper project that fits your creative needs.

We also offer editing and writing services, writing classes, journal and planner making, bullet journaling, bookbinding, papermaking, and marbling.

Bring your fragments. Let’s see what they can become.

04 / ABOUT & CV

A writer.
A teacher.
But always a maker.

I’m Sam Andrus-Henry (published as Samantha Andrus-Henry), a graduate student in Writing and Rhetoric Studies at the University of Utah, an educator, and a mixed-media paper artist.

I keep coming back to the same questions: How do people make meaning? What helps them participate? And what might we notice if we paid more attention to the work along the way?

My background in composition, instructional design, communication, and student mentoring informs both my scholarship and my creative practice. I’m especially drawn to graduate education and mentoring, community college and open-access education, reading and writing as craft, lifecycling, visual rhetoric, and the design of usable learning environments.

I like work that crosses boundaries, is fragmentary, and fits in patches: notebooks that become archives of thinking spaces, annotations that are conversations, and course pages that help students make their selves and next steps.
